# Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) Cholesterol
## Summary
Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is one of the five major groups of lipoproteins that transport fat molecules around the body in extracellular water. Elevated LDL is an established causal factor for the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Normal non-atherogenic LDL-C levels are between 20-40 mg/dl, and guidelines recommend maintaining LDL-C under 2.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dl) and under 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) for those at high risk. LDL delivers fat molecules to cells and is formed when triglycerides are removed from VLDL by the lipoprotein lipase enzyme.
## Key Concepts
* LDL: Low-density lipoprotein
* VLDL: Very low-density lipoprotein
* HDL: High-density lipoprotein
* Apolipoprotein B: A protein that has 4536 amino acid residues and a mass of 514 kDa
* Lipoprotein lipase enzyme (LPL): An enzyme that removes triglycerides from VLDL
* LDL receptors: Receptors that bind to LDL particles in the bloodstream
* PCSK9: A proprotein convertase that marks the LDL receptor for degradation
## Details
LDL particles are complex particles composed of multiple proteins, typically 80-100 proteins per particle, and are approximately 22-27.5 nanometers in diameter. They transport 3,000 to 6,000 fat molecules per particle and vary in size according to the number and mix of fat molecules contained within. LDL delivers fat molecules to cells by binding to LDL receptors on the cell surface, which then undergo receptor-mediated endocytosis. Elevated LDL is an established causal factor for the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and normal non-atherogenic LDL-C levels are between 20-40 mg/dl.
